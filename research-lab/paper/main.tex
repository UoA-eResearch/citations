\begin{abstract}
\noindent AI agents can now choose a research question, analyse data and write up a result. Evidence on how such
research goes wrong is accumulating as fast: post-hoc selection, overclaimed significance, underpowered nulls
presented as findings, AI reviewers that accept work they have flagged, and large variation between agent analysts.

We describe a protocol that applies the safeguards of confirmatory science to an autonomous research agent, and audit
how it worked in practice. One AI agent:
\begin{itemize}
\item scouts and scores \LeadsTotal{} leads in \LeadDomains{} fields;
\item commits a preregistration to a public repository before analysing outcome data;
\item keeps outcomes sealed where it can;
\item validates its methods;
\item obtains an adversarial review from a separate model instance before any verdict;
\item logs every deviation with a timestamp;
\item publishes plain-language reports with one of five verdicts.
\end{itemize}

We audited the first \StudiesTotal{} studies, in \StudyDomains{} fields, using git history, two independent coder
agents of different models, automated number tracing and reference resolution.

\textbf{Verdicts.} Of \VerdictsDecided{} decided verdicts, \VerdictSupported{} support the preregistered hypothesis,
\VerdictRefuted{} refute it, \VerdictUnsupported{} are unsupported and \VerdictInconclusive{} are inconclusive.

\textbf{Preregistration.} Plans demonstrably preceded outcomes in \PreregPassClassified{} of \StudiesTotal{} studies.
In the lab's first study, preregistration cannot be verified from the public record.

\textbf{Deviations and review.} \DevVerdictRelevantPct{} of \DevTotal{} logged deviations could have changed a
verdict. Independent review changed \ReviewHeadlineChanged{} headline estimates and \ReviewVerdictChanged{} verdicts.

\textbf{Weaknesses.} Safeguards were uneven. Only one plan stated a power calculation, and only \InvBlindStrong{}
studies kept outcomes sealed or not yet in existence. Naive tracing of report numbers to result files proved nearly
uninformative: \NumChanceAll{} of numbers ``traced'' to unrelated studies.

\textbf{References.} All \RefArxiv{} arXiv identifiers and \RefDoi{} DOIs cited resolve.

We release the protocol, plans, reports, code and audit data, and propose eight changes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI agents can now carry out every step of an empirical study: choosing a question, finding data, writing and running
analysis code, interpreting results and drafting a paper
\citep{lu2024aiscientist,yamada2025aiscientistv2,kosmos2025,denario2025,agon2026,ifargan2025datatopaper}.

These systems raise an old problem in a new form. Confirmatory science relies on fixing a question and an analysis
before seeing the answer, because otherwise flexible choices can manufacture findings
\citep{simmons2011false,gelman2014garden,nosek2018prereg}. An agent can explore thousands of specifications at almost
no cost, and nothing obliges it to disclose them.

Recent work documents concrete failures:
\begin{itemize}
\item AI scientist systems select benchmarks and runs post hoc, and audits miss this without the full logs
\citep{pitfalls2025};
\item frontier agents give underpowered negative results the status of findings \citep{kirgis2026};
\item the interpretive statements in an AI scientist's reports are much less accurate than its data-analysis
statements \citep{kosmos2025};
\item AI analysts given the same data and question reach opposite conclusions
\citep{bertran2026many,gao2026nse,forkingpaths2026};
\item AI reviewers can be steered into accepting unsound work \citep{badscientist2025,agents4science2025}.
\end{itemize}

A recent survey finds that most systems release code but few release the traces needed to verify a claim
\citep{verificationgap2026}.

\paragraph{This paper.} We describe and audit a research lab run by a single AI agent under a protocol borrowed from
confirmatory science. Under it, preregistration is written by the agent itself and committed publicly before outcomes
are analysed. The protocol was used for \StudiesTotal{} studies in \StudyDomains{} fields between September and
October 2026, all on open data, and all plans, code, deviation logs and reports are public. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a description of the protocol, from lead generation to publication, at a level that allows reuse;
\item a self-audit of how the protocol worked in practice, designed in advance (the audit plan was committed before any
measure was computed) and coded by independent agents of two different models;
\item the failures the audit found, reported in the main text, and changes that follow from them.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What is not claimed.} We do not claim that the studies' conclusions are correct; no external domain expert
has checked them. Nor do we claim that the protocol prevents all of the failures above. The audit measures whether the
protocol's commitments were kept and what its safeguards caught.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Autonomous research systems.} Several systems cover several fields or the full research cycle:
\begin{itemize}
\item Kosmos runs hundreds of agent rollouts around a shared world model, across metabolomics, materials, neuroscience
and genetics. Independent scientists judged 79.4\% of its report statements accurate, but only 57.9\% of its
interpretive ones \citep{kosmos2025}.
\item Denario produced papers in more than ten disciplines \citep{denario2025}.
\item Agon runs an ``omnidisciplinary'' loop and contributes a taxonomy of its own failures \citep{agon2026}.
\item data-to-paper links every number in a generated paper to the code that produced it \citep{ifargan2025datatopaper}.
\item Ai2's CodeScientist and AutoDiscovery search open-endedly for findings \citep{codescientist2025,autods2025}.
\item The AI Scientist and ScientistTwo automate machine-learning research
\citep{lu2024aiscientist,yamada2025aiscientistv2,scientisttwo2026}.
\end{itemize}
Biomni executes biomedical workflows \citep{biomni2026}. None of these systems preregisters its studies. Most start
from a topic chosen by a human, and success is usually reported as a set of discoveries rather than as a distribution
of verdicts.

\paragraph{Evidence on failure.} We summarised the main empirical findings above
\citep{pitfalls2025,kirgis2026,si2025gap,bertran2026many,gao2026nse,forkingpaths2026,badscientist2025,agents4science2025}.
Two more strands are relevant.
\begin{itemize}
\item Ideas that LLMs generate look more novel than human ideas but lose that advantage once executed
\citep{si2025gap}. This bears directly on any system that ranks its own leads.
\item Open health surveys have been mined for formulaic single-factor association papers with no false-discovery
control \citep{suchak2025nhanes}, a risk for any open-data research engine.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Preregistration and audit for AI research.} Preregistration has been proposed for experiments \emph{on} AI
agents \citep{vaccaro2026prereg}, including against LLMs not yet released \citep{thomas2026next}. Several audit
protocols are closer to ours:
\begin{itemize}
\item CrossAudit records audits as git commits, with reviewers from a different vendor
\citep{crossaudit2026};
\item the Discovery Certification Protocol tests whether a claimed discovery can be recovered without the search
history \citep{dcp2026};
\item an auditable agent loop for empirical economics evaluates on a hold-out built after the search \citep{shin2026auditable};
\item ABE-Ralph audits whether experiments faithfully test their claims \citep{abe2026fidelity}.
\end{itemize}
Each was demonstrated on one or two cases or on seeded defects. Case studies of open-source research frameworks found
that none completed a full research cycle \citep{agrawal2026case}.

As far as we know, this is the first protocol in which an autonomous agent preregisters its own studies across a
portfolio of fields, and the first audit of such a portfolio.

\section{The protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The lab runs on one workstation (32-core Xeon, 94\,GB RAM, one A100 80\,GB GPU). The agent is Claude (Anthropic),
working in a long-running Claude Code session. Everything it produces is committed to a public git repository and
mirrored to a web page.

The human lab owner sets the programme, authorises resource use (for example, stopping a shared GPU service), and makes
decisions the agent flags as theirs (for example, whether to proceed without M\=aori and Pacific input on a study of
an ethnicity-based waitlist policy). They did not write any plan, code or report.

\subsection{Leads: scouting and triage}

Leads come from three scouting rounds run as multi-agent workflows: 10 domain scouts and a completeness critic; 8
scouts and a calibration critic; and a local round on Auckland and Aotearoa New Zealand. They are stored in
\texttt{leads.json}. Each lead records:
\begin{itemize}
\item a falsifiable hypothesis;
\item the gap it fills;
\item literature for and against;
\item open datasets with access notes;
\item a method and step-by-step plan;
\item risks;
\item a value score (1--10), a cost score (1--10) and a feasibility class;
\item a critic's note;
\item a one-paragraph plain-language summary.
\end{itemize}

There are \LeadsTotal{} leads in \LeadDomains{} fields. An interactive value-against-cost plot is published, and the
agent works through leads in the high-value, low-cost quadrant. Before a lead is executed it gets a fresh novelty
check; one lead was dropped at this stage because a 2026 paper had already answered its question.

\subsection{Preregistration}

Before analysing outcome data, the agent writes \texttt{plan.md} and commits and pushes it. The plan contains:
\begin{itemize}
\item the question and hypothesis;
\item a disclosure of what has already been seen;
\item data and units;
\item the primary statistic;
\item a decision rule mapping results to a verdict;
\item validation steps;
\item secondary analyses, labelled as such.
\end{itemize}

The verdicts are \emph{Supported}, \emph{Refuted}, \emph{Unsupported}, \emph{Inconclusive} and \emph{Pending} (for
prospective tests). The rule must say when the evidence is too weak to decide, so that a null result is not reported
as a refutation.

\subsection{Keeping outcomes from the analyst}

Where the design allows, outcomes are kept out of the agent's reach until the analysis is fixed:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sealed.} Outcome data are stored unread and only their SHA-256 hash is recorded. Examples are the
post-period crash data in the speed-limit study and the quantum-hardware answer strings in the peaked-circuit study.
\item \textbf{Prospective.} The outcome does not yet exist when the plan is committed. Examples are City Rail Link
ridership through March 2027 and future forecast targets.
\item \textbf{Formal.} The outcome is a machine-checked certificate, as with Lean kernel proofs in the formal-mathematics
study.
\end{itemize}
Otherwise the plan's disclosure states which outcome data the agent had already seen. How often each mechanism was
used is part of the audit (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Validation before analysis}

Methods are checked before they are used: on synthetic data with known truth, against positive and negative controls,
or by reproducing a published number. If validation fails, the method is fixed or the claim is downgraded, and the
change is logged.

\subsection{Independent adversarial review}

Before any verdict, a separate agent instance reviews the run. In most studies this is Claude Fable~5.1, a different
model from the executing agent. It is told to try to refute the result, and it sees the plan, code, results and draft
report. It returns a recommendation, for example ``fix first''. Required fixes are made and logged. When the review
says ``fix first'', the same reviewer instance then checks the fixes in a confirmation pass.

\subsection{Deviation log and reporting}

\texttt{deviations.md} records every departure from the plan, with a timestamp from the system clock. Each report
contains:
\begin{itemize}
\item an ``In plain terms'' section of 150--300 words;
\item what was done;
\item results;
\item caveats;
\item a summary of the review.
\end{itemize}
The verdict, a one-line note and a headline are written back to \texttt{leads.json} and the public page.

\section{Self-audit design}
\label{sec:audit}

The audit protocol was written and committed before any measure was computed (commit \texttt{f424e29}). The agent
that ran the studies also wrote this paper, so judgement-based coding was given to two independent coder agents of
different models (Claude Fable~5.1 and Claude Opus). They saw only the study files and a fixed codebook, and did not
see each other's work. A third coder (Opus) made an inventory of safeguards in each plan; this was added after the
protocol and logged as a deviation of the audit.

The measures were:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(A)] whether git history shows each plan committed before the first outcome file;
\item[(B)] coding of every deviation entry by category, trigger, timing and whether it could change the verdict;
\item[(C)] what independent review raised and what it changed;
\item[(D)] automated tracing of every number in each report to the study's result files, with a chance-match control
added after the protocol;
\item[(E)] resolution of every cited arXiv identifier and DOI;
\item[(F)] scout estimates against outcomes.
\end{itemize}
Agreement is Cohen's $\kappa$ \citep{cohen1960kappa}. Departures from the audit protocol are logged in
\path{paper/audit/deviations.md}.

\section*{AI contribution statement}

An AI agent (Claude, Anthropic; Opus- and Fable-class models) carried out all of the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item generated and scored the leads;
\item wrote every plan, all code, every deviation log and every report;
\item carried out the audit, with independent coder agents for the judgement-based measures;
\item drafted this paper.
\end{itemize}
An independent agent reviewed the paper before release (Appendix~\ref{app:review}).

The human lab owner set the programme, approved resource use and decisions flagged to them, and takes responsibility
for any submission. No human checked the scientific conclusions of the individual studies.
