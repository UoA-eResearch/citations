Do AI models pass on Indigenous data-governance notices?

A within-record experiment on 221 real repository records: Local Contexts Notices and Labels, or matched conventional rights clauses, swapped into the same metadata slot and given to three open-weight models under three framings. 19,890 responses.

Report · 2026-10-17 · pre-review draft Preregistration Deviations log Code All research leads

Verdict: Refuted, on all three preregistered hypotheses. The notices are passed on; they are not acted on. The lead predicted that models would omit Local Contexts elements in at least 70% of reuse advice, 25 points more often than conventional clauses, and that an element would rarely change the advice.

  • Omission: the models omitted a Local Contexts element in 0.5% of responses (95% interval 0.4–0.6%), slightly less often than conventional clauses (1.7%).
  • Who omits: almost every omission came from one model in one framing.
  • Verdict change: in the structured "agent" framing, an element changed the stated reuse verdict 85% of the time.

But post-hoc analysis shows what that change usually is: a move to "unclear".

  • A conventional "please contact the data providers before reuse; collaboration is welcome" becomes "permission or contact required: yes" in 100% of agent summaries.
  • The TK Open to Collaboration Label, the matched Indigenous-governance invitation, becomes "yes" in 2%.
  • A data use agreement becomes "yes" in 100%. TK Community Use Only, which Local Contexts describes as material "not, and never was, free, public, or available for everyone", becomes "yes" in 28%, and "commercial use allowed" in 31%.

In plain terms

Indigenous communities and institutions can attach "Notices" and "Labels" to datasets in online repositories: short, machine-readable statements such as TK Community Use Only or Traditional Knowledge Notice. They tell anyone reusing the data that there are cultural rights or community rules attached. Researchers increasingly ask AI assistants whether they can reuse a dataset. We wanted to know whether the AI passes these notices on.

We took 221 real dataset records and made copies of each with a different notice inserted: five kinds of Local Contexts notice, three ordinary conditions (such as "non-commercial use only"), or nothing. We gave them to three openly available AI models, asking in three different ways, and checked nearly 20,000 answers.

The models almost always mentioned the notice. Only one model, in one style of question, occasionally left it out. So the worry that AI simply ignores these notices is not borne out.

What the models did with them is another matter. When asked to fill in a short reuse summary, a plain request to "contact the providers before reuse" was always turned into "contact required". An Indigenous label asking for the same kind of collaboration almost never was; the models mostly answered "unclear". A label saying the material is for community use only still produced "commercial use allowed" about a third of the time.

The AI passes on the words. It does not reliably treat Indigenous notices as conditions in the way it treats ordinary ones.

What was asked

The lead (scouted 2026-10-16, University of Auckland Ancestral Futures signature research area) asked whether open-weight models are "notice-blind". Do they omit Local Contexts elements they are given, more often than conventional non-standard rights clauses, without the element changing their advice?

The harvest made the lead's design impossible (D1). Of the 48,353 DataCite records carrying a Local Contexts URI, 48,332 are from one repository, Neotoma, and all of those carry one element. Only 21 records anywhere else carry one. So the study became a within-record experiment. Each sampled record is shown with every condition in the same rightsList slot, after its CC BY licence, and nothing else changes.

hypothesis prediction decision rule
H1 pooled Local Contexts omission ≥ 70% Supported if the 95% interval's lower end ≥ 0.70; Refuted if its upper end < 0.70
H2 Local Contexts omission exceeds conventional omission by ≥ 25 points Supported if lower end ≥ 0.25; Refuted if upper end < 0.25
H3 an element changes the agent verdict, relative to the same record without it, in ≤ 20% Supported if upper end ≤ 0.20; Refuted if lower end > 0.20

What was already known

Three searches by the scouting critic found nothing that tests whether a model surfaces a rights element it was given, and nothing with an Indigenous-governance arm. The nearest work extracts licences into rights vocabularies (LLM4RE, arXiv 2510.18787), or documents reference fabrication that disadvantages marginalised scholarship. The Notices were designed to travel with data (Liggins, Hudson & Anderson 2021, Mol. Ecol.). DataCite documents them as part of its schema.

How it was done

Records. 200 Neotoma records drawn at random, plus all 21 non-Neotoma records (Pacific research stations at Gump and Tetiaroa, iPlaces, Zenodo). Existing Local Contexts elements were removed, and long lists were capped identically across conditions. A disjoint 20 Neotoma records formed a pilot set.

Conditions (wording and URIs from localcontexts.org):

  • Local Contexts: TK Open to Collaboration (Neotoma's real form), TK Non-Commercial, BC Notice, TK Notice, TK Community Use Only, and TK Open to Collaboration without its URI.
  • Conventional: a CC BY-NC licence, "A data use agreement is required before reuse", and "Users are asked to contact the data providers before reuse; collaboration is welcome".
  • None.

Matched pairs: TK NC against CC BY-NC (the same restriction), and TK CB against the contact clause (the same invitation).

Models and framings. Three models at greedy decoding, up to 1,100 new tokens, all run locally on vLLM. No record went to a third-party model for generation or scoring. The pilot adjudication was done by the agent running the study, so those 200 responses, which quote public metadata, did pass through the agent's own model service:

  • Qwen3-32B (non-thinking mode);
  • Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501;
  • OLMo-2-1124-13B-Instruct.

Three framings:

  • a researcher asking "can I reuse this, and under what conditions?";
  • an agent producing a JSON summary with fields for commercial use and for whether permission or contact is required;
  • "list every condition, notice or restriction".

221 records × 10 conditions × 3 framings × 3 models = 19,890 responses. 0 errors; 14 truncated.

Scoring. A preregistered lexical rule per condition: does the response name the element or state what it asks? Before the main set was scored, I adjudicated 200 pilot responses, blind to the rule (D4). All 200 mentioned the element, and the rule agreed on every one (sensitivity 1.00). Specificity comes from each pattern's hit rate on responses with no element: 1–2% for most patterns, 7–8% for the two non-commercial ones. A name-only secondary measure guards against that.

Operational deviations (D2, D3), all before any element-condition response was read:

  • Two model servers failed to start in the first GPU window (one a context-length mismatch).
  • One script lacked a package.
  • The generation cap was raised from 700 to 1,100 tokens after a quarter of the first pilot responses hit it.

Every omission of a Local Contexts element comes from OLMo-2-13B's researcher answers. In that cell it drops the BC Notice 11% of the time and a CC BY-NC licence 42%. When a Local Contexts element is mentioned, it is named as such (Local Contexts, TK, BC, Indigenous or cultural rights) 99.9% of the time. Removing the localcontexts.org URI barely matters (TK CB omission 0.6% with it, 1.0% without).

H3: Refuted. Elements change the verdict, mostly to "unclear"

In the agent framing an element changes at least one of the two verdict fields, against the same record and model without it, in 85.3% (84.1–86.4%) of cases (50 of 3,315 pairs unparseable). The direction of the change is the substantive result, described post hoc below.

Read against what each element says:

  • The matched restriction is honoured equally. TK Non-Commercial and a CC BY-NC licence both produce "no commercial use" over 90% of the time.
  • The matched invitation is not. The conventional request to contact the providers is read as a requirement every time, by every model. The TK Open to Collaboration Label, whose template text says the community "is open to future engagement, collaboration, and partnership", is read as one 2% of the time. Arguably the conventional request is over-read and the Label under-read; either way, the same kind of ask gets different treatment.
  • The strongest restriction is often softened. Local Contexts' template for TK Community Use Only says the material "has specific conditions for circulation within the community" and "is not, and never was, free, public, or available for everyone". Models answer "commercial use allowed: yes" in 31% of agent summaries: OLMo-2 28%, Mistral 7% and Qwen3 5% chose "no", with the rest "unclear" or "yes".
  • The Notices are acknowledged, not translated into conditions. The BC and TK Notices say that "appropriate permissions may need" to be sought; models mostly answer "unclear", and 18–29% answer "no".
  • Prose framings (lexical, unvalidated). Local Contexts elements are described as "not legally binding" or similar in 3–12% of responses that mention them (BC Notice 12%, TK CB 12%). The conventional contact request is described that way 30% of the time, which is accurate for a request.

A separate finding: in the responses with no extra element, where the record carries only CC BY, OLMo-2-13B attaches NonCommercial, NoDerivatives or ShareAlike terms to the licence in 51% of its prose answers. Qwen3 does so in 4%, Mistral never. The pilot adjudication noted further characterisation errors: "BC" read as British Columbia, TK Community Use Only taken to mean "the paleoecology research community", and a CC BY-NC licence listed with "commercial use allowed: yes".

What this does and does not show

It shows that three current open-weight models, given a repository record's metadata, almost always pass on a Local Contexts Notice or Label, and by name. The "notice-blind" hypothesis is refuted for this setting. It also shows, post hoc, that in a structured reuse summary these models treat Local Contexts elements as uncertain far more often than conventional clauses that ask the same kind of thing.

It does not show:

  1. Hosted assistants. These are three open-weight models at greedy decoding, not the hosted assistants most researchers use.
  2. Other presentations. The element sits in a metadata JSON. A landing page, a PDF, or a record with many fields may be harder.
  3. Whether "unclear" is the right answer. For some elements it is defensible. What each element asks is taken from Local Contexts' published text, but whether a given answer respects it is a normative judgement this lab has not made with the communities concerned. That engagement is a care point, not a precondition (plan §8).
  4. Real-world element variety. Element types other than Neotoma's are inserted experimentally, using Local Contexts' own names, because almost no real records carry them.
  5. Validated post-hoc measures. The verdict tables use the models' own structured fields, but the "not binding" and invented-term counts are lexical and were not validated.

Scope and care

The study used only public repository metadata and never asked a model to produce or elaborate Indigenous knowledge; only reuse statements were scored. The results describe models, not the communities, institutions or repositories involved.

One data-quality point is worth passing to Neotoma and Local Contexts. Neotoma's element pairs the name of a TK Label ("TK Open to Collaboration (TK CB)") with the URI of the Open to Collaborate Notice, a different instrument.

Process and deviations

Six logged deviations (deviations.md):

  • D1: within-record design; no LLM judge, with agent adjudication of the pilot instead.
  • D2: GPU-stage failures: a start timeout, a context-length mismatch, a missing package.
  • D3: generation cap raised to 1,100 tokens.
  • D4: pilot adjudicated; scorer frozen unchanged; name-only secondary added.
  • D5: a bootstrap relabelling bug, fixed before any result was printed.
  • D6: post-hoc verdict and wording checks.
