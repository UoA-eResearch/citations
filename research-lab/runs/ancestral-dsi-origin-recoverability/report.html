Countries of origin on sequence samples: the 2023 INSDC rule left the usable-country share unresolved, and most untagged samples have no origin to recover

Every non-human sample submitted to NCBI BioSample from June 2021 to May 2025 (20.9 million) was classified by its country field. A regression discontinuity tested the June 2023 INSDC rule. Then a 500-record gold set tested whether the missing countries can be recovered automatically from the sample's own record, its BioProject and its linked publication. This version incorporates an independent review (`review/review.md`; changes logged as D10).

Report · 2026-10-23 · revised after independent review Preregistration Deviations log Code All research leads

Verdict: H1 Inconclusive; H2 Refuted.

  • H1 (the rule raised the usable-country share by less than 10 points): Inconclusive.
    • The jump at 1 June 2023 is +2.9 points (95% interval −5.9 to +10.8). The upper bound crosses the 10-point line.
    • Records after the rule more often carry a placeholder ("missing", "not collected" and so on) where earlier ones were blank: placeholders +9.1 points (+1.8 to +18.2), absent fields −12.0 (−16.0 to −8.7).
    • That shift is a trend visible across the rule, not an effect the design can attribute to it. At 55 placebo cutoffs, placeholder jumps have a standard deviation of 5.5 points, and 9 of 55 are as large as the real one (review).
    • The raw usable share actually fell across the cutoff, from 85.3% to 79.4%. The +2.9 estimate is the local-linear extrapolation's.
    • Within submitters active on both sides, the usable share barely moved (+0.6; −4.1 to +8.8).
    • Placebo dates a year and two years earlier show jumps of +17.6 and −15.7 points. The series swings too much for this design to measure a 10-point effect precisely.
  • H2 (an automatic method recovers a correct country for at least 40% of untagged samples at 90% precision): Refuted.
    • Only 19 of the 300 test records (6.3%) have a country that any evidence states, as an explicit sampling site. No method could reach 40%.
    • At their tuned thresholds the three methods recover 0%. The upper bounds of their intervals are 1.3%.
  • Why: most untagged samples have no origin to find.
    • Projected to the 1.66 million untagged samples submitted from June 2023 to May 2025:
      • 61% are laboratory or cultivated material (lab mice, cell lines, reference strains, cultivars), for which no collection origin applies or none is recorded;
      • 32% are natural samples whose origin no evidence states;
      • 8% (about 127,000; 81,000 to 173,000) have a stated sampling country somewhere in the evidence. Some of these already carry it, under a different field name.
    • Samples whose BioProject links a publication are 92% laboratory material.
  • Correction after review. Nine gold labels had taken a country from a collector's address or an experimental facility. The plan excludes both. Relabelled, the ceiling falls from 8% to 6.3%, and the one method that had recovered anything (1.7%) recovers nothing (D10).

In plain terms

When a scientist deposits a DNA sample from a plant, an animal or a microbe, the record is supposed to say which country it came from. International rules on sharing the benefits of genetic resources depend on that: you cannot pay a country back for a resource if nobody recorded where it came from. In 2023 the world's DNA archives made the country field compulsory.

We checked 20.9 million sample records. Over these years, fewer records left the field blank and more wrote "missing" or "not collected" instead, but this was a gradual trend we cannot pin on the rule. The share naming a real country did not clearly rise.

Then we asked whether the missing countries could be filled in automatically, by reading the sample's description and the paper it belongs to. Mostly they cannot, because there is nothing to find. Six in ten of the samples without a country were laboratory animals, cell lines, lab strains or crop cultivars, for which no place of collection applies or none is recorded. Most of the rest never say where they were collected, anywhere. Only about one in thirteen had a sampling country written down somewhere else in their records. A language model found most of those when allowed to guess, but it also guessed countries for lab samples, usually the country of the lab. Held to the 90% accuracy the plan required, no method made any calls.

What was asked

The lead (ancestral-dsi-origin-recoverability) asked two things: - whether the INSDC's spatiotemporal metadata rule (in force 1 June 2023) put countries of origin on sequence samples; - whether the origins still missing can be recovered at archive scale.

This matters for the Convention on Biological Diversity's arrangements on digital sequence information, including the Cali Fund: traceability to a provider country starts with a country on the record. The plan (plan.md) was written before the archive was parsed.

How it was done

  1. Classification (parse.py, countries.py). Each BioSample's geo_loc_name (or country) is:
    • usable if its country part resolves to an INSDC country or ocean;
    • placeholder if it is an INSDC missing-value term or a common equivalent;
    • malformed or absent otherwise.

Human samples and human-host metagenomes are removed (care filter, D3). D4 fixed an apostrophe bug that had classed "Cote d'Ivoire" malformed (10,183 samples reclassified). 2. Limb 1 (analysis_rd.py). A local-linear regression discontinuity on daily shares at 1 June 2023: - 24 months either side, with a one-month donut; - intervals from a cluster bootstrap over 43,055 submitting organisations; - secondaries: the 1 January 2025 extension, placebo dates, bandwidths, no donut, package fixed effects, and placeholder terms for inapplicable material removed; - post hoc (analysis_rd_posthoc.py): without SARS-CoV-2, and within submitters. 3. The gold set (goldset.py). 500 untagged samples from June 2023 to May 2025, half with a linked publication, stratified by package; 200 tuning and 300 test. - Evidence: the sample's attributes, its BioProject title and description, and for a publication its title, abstract and up to 12 sampling sentences from the open-access full text. - Never evidence: submitter institutions. 4. Labels. Each record is a country, inapplicable (laboratory material) or not stated (D2). - Two blind passes by the study's AI agent, more than five hours apart, each in its own shuffled order. - A blind third pass on the disagreements, under the plan's fixed rule (D5). - Three records are excluded by code: two human-host metagenomes, and one whose own country field resolves after D4. That leaves 497. 5. Recovery arms (arms.py), run only after all labels were fixed: - a GeoNames gazetteer with reverse geocoding of coordinates; - a TF-IDF classifier trained on 196,561 tagged samples from the same period; - the locally served open-weight LLM (Nemotron 3 Nano Omni), asked for the collection country or "unknown", with its stated probability as confidence (D6: output budget raised before running).

Each arm's threshold is the lowest giving 90% precision on the tuning split. A call on a record without a determinable country counts as wrong.

The gold set (results/figures/F2_gold_and_recovery.png)

  • Agreement (gold_agreement.json).
    • The two passes agree with Cohen's κ = 0.93 on the full labels, and κ = 0.94 on what scoring uses (a country versus not determinable). That is 17 disagreements, 4 of which change a scoring outcome.
    • One agent made both passes in one working session (D5), so these figures are upper bounds on what independent labellers would reach.
    • The third pass gave 3 of the 17 a country.
  • Final labels after D10 (497 records):
    • inapplicable 350 (70%), not stated 119 (24%), country 28 (6%);
    • with a linked publication: 92% inapplicable, 7% not stated, 1% country;
    • without: 49% inapplicable, 41% not stated, 10% country.
    • "Inapplicable" means no collection origin applies or none is recorded for lab-propagated material. 234 of the 350 are Mus musculus. 21 are field, agricultural or genebank material with no stated source (review), so the category is not a clean biological partition.
  • The 28 countries: China 7, USA 6, Japan 2, the Pacific Ocean 2, and one each for 11 others.
    • By the CBD: 64% are Parties, 21% are not (the USA), and 14% are oceans.
    • Where the submitter's country is known (10 records), it equals the sample's country in 7.
  • Labels as first adjudicated (*_pre_d10 tables): 37 countries, including 12 USA (6 from a US state code in a collector field), Spain (an experimental facility) and China (a germplasm grow-out site).
  • Projected to the 1,657,423 untagged window samples (stratified by publication link):
    • inapplicable 60.7% (56.1–65.3%);
    • not stated 31.6% (27.1–36.2%);
    • country 7.7% (4.9–10.4%), about 127,000 samples (81,000–173,000). Before D10 this was 9.2%.
  • Some of that 7.7% is already recorded. Besides geo_loc_name, a country can sit under "geographic location, country and/or sea" (2,190 window samples, D3) or geo_location (a further 4,445, found in review). Two of the 28 gold countries are of this kind, so part of the share is recorded under another field name rather than recoverable by mining.
  • Full-text check (D8). Each paper contributed at most 12 sampling sentences, and every open-access paper hit that cap. The 12 publication-linked "not stated" records were checked against their full texts: none gives a single sampling country. Even if every one had, the projected country share would rise by at most 1.3 points.

  • Before D10 the classifier reached 90% on two tuning calls (threshold 0.971) and recovered 1.7% (0.7–3.8%) at 100% precision. One of those two tuning calls was R475, a label D10 removes.
  • At any confidence on the test split:
    • gazetteer: 4.0% correct, at 12.6% precision;
    • classifier: 4.7% correct, at 4.7% precision (it always names a country);
    • LLM: 5.3% correct (16 of 300), at 57.1% precision (16 of 28 calls). It finds 16 of the 19 test records that have a country.
  • Two discarded answers. The LLM arm discarded two confident answers that fell outside the INSDC vocabulary: R357, and R401 (a test record whose gold is the Pacific Ocean). Raw answers were not kept, so this slightly understates its recall.
  • The ceiling is the binding constraint. With 19 determinable records in 300, even a perfect arm would recover 6.3%.

  • Asymmetric error (secondary).

    • Where an arm names the wrong country and the submitter's country is known, it names the submitter's country in (test split, predictions at any confidence, since after D10 no arm has a tuned threshold):
      • 75% of the LLM's errors (6 of 8; 41–93%);
      • 60% of the classifier's (52–67%);
      • 38% of the gazetteer's (25–52%).

    Over all 497 records the shares are 64.7% (11 of 17), 57.7% and 34.2%. - Most of these errors are calls on laboratory material, such as naming the lab's country for lab mice. INSDC's own vocabulary has "missing: lab stock" for exactly this case. - Calibration (Brier score, all records): gazetteer 0.75, classifier 0.44, LLM 0.50. The LLM states 0.95–1.00 for many wrong calls.

Post hoc: natural samples only (D7; results/figures/F3_posthoc_natural.png)

  • The ceiling. Removing laboratory material leaves 147 gold records, of which 19% (14–26%) have a findable country.
  • The LLM names a country for 35 of them and is right on 23 (66% precision; 49–79%), finding 82% of the determinable ones.
  • The gazetteer and classifier reach 29% and 12% precision.
  • So once laboratory material is filtered out, an LLM finds most of what can be found, but still short of 90% precision.

Mirror agreement (plan §5; mirror_agreement.json)

A random 10,000 window samples were looked up in the ENA portal; 9,729 were found. - Country. Whether a sample has a usable country agrees in 99.9% of them. Where both mirrors have one (8,079), it is the same country every time. - Absence. The mirrors record it differently. Among the found samples, BioSample holds 829 placeholders and 806 absent fields; ENA holds 525 and 1,111. So part of what limb 1 counts as placeholders appears in ENA as a blank. - Conclusion. The usable-country results do not depend on which mirror is read. The placeholder/absent split does: 304 of 829 BioSample placeholders (37%) are plain blanks in ENA. This is another reason not to treat the placeholder shift as a measured effect.

What this does and does not show

  • Neither change can be attributed to the rule.
    • Over 2019–2025, blanks gave way to placeholders, which the rule asks for when a country cannot be given. But the change was gradual, and the design's own placebos produce jumps as large as the one at the rule.
    • Whether the usable share rose by less than 10 points cannot be settled with this design: the series moves by more than that in years with no rule change.
  • The traceability gap is mostly not a recovery problem.
    • About 61% of untagged samples are laboratory material, whose missing country is legitimate.
    • About 32% are natural samples whose origin is recorded nowhere we looked.
    • About one in thirteen states a sampling country somewhere in its evidence, and some of those already carry it under another field.
    • Improving traceability for provider countries therefore means recording origin at collection. Mining records afterwards cannot supply it.
  • This contrasts with an earlier estimate.
    • The CBD's traceability study found that 44% of checked untagged GenBank entries state an origin in the linked publication. Here, 3 of 250 publication-linked untagged BioSamples (1.2%) have a stated sampling country in any evidence, because 92% of them are laboratory material.
    • The populations differ (GenBank sequence entries against BioSample records, which are dominated by expression studies of lab organisms), and so does the evidence (abstracts and up to 12 sampling sentences of open-access full text, not the whole paper).
  • Limits.
    • All labelling was done by the study's AI agent, in two passes and a third, with no human adjudicator.
    • The evidence is what the archive stores and the open-access literature holds. It excludes closed papers, supplementary tables, and attributes past a 600-character cap.
    • Laboratory animals, their housing facilities and experimental sites were not treated as having an origin (D5, D10). A different construct, "where was this sample taken", would put a country on many of them, and then the arms' "errors" on lab material would count as recoveries.
    • The gold set was drawn from the classification before D3/D4, while the projection counts the population after them. The effect is well under a point.
