Do AI autoformalizers copy the known mistakes in miniF2F and ProofNet?

A preregistered error-copying test. Open autoformalizers were given benchmark problems whose official Lean statements have documented mistakes, and number-perturbed twins of the same problems, and the Lean kernel decided which outputs reproduce the mistake.

Verdict: Refuted. Three open autoformalizers reproduce the benchmarks' documented defects in 7.2% of samples on the original problems and the analogous defect in 7.9% on number-perturbed twins. That is a rate ratio of 0.91 (95% CI 0.76-1.06), where the hypothesis needed at least 3. So the copying is not recall tied to a problem's numbers. It is not evidence against memorized benchmark statements, though. One model, Kimina, reproduces the ProofNet Lean port's header lines and, on one problem, a wrong constant that only the port contains, on twins as often as on originals. That is memory of a statement's template, which this design cannot detect.

In plain terms

AI "autoformalizers" translate a maths problem written in words into Lean, a language a computer can check. Two benchmarks they are tested on, miniF2F and ProofNet, have official Lean versions that were later found to contain many mistakes, such as a missing condition or a claim weaker than the problem's. A model that had memorised those versions might reproduce their mistakes.

We tested 59 such problems that contain numbers. For each we made a twin with different numbers and carried the mistake over to the twin's official-style version. A model recalling an official statement together with its numbers should copy the mistake on the original but not on the twin. A model falling into a common trap should make it on both.

Three autoformalizers wrote 16 versions of every problem and every twin. The Lean checker certified which ones say the same thing as the flawed official statement: 7.2% of attempts on originals and 7.9% on twins. The prediction was at least three times as often on originals. It is refuted.

That rules out recall tied to the numbers, but not memory of a statement's shape. One model, Kimina, shows clear signs of that. In about half of its answers to ProofNet problems it writes the exact header lines of the official ProofNet Lean files, which nothing in the problem asks for. On one problem it repeats a wrong constant from the official version on the twin as well. The other four models never write those headers.

Rewording the problems cut Kimina's copied mistakes by half, where changing the numbers did not. Herald's fell too, though on one problem only because the reworded text led it to write something Lean rejects.

What was done

The defect catalogue

Pairs of statements. Each candidate item pairs a defective original formal statement D with a corrected reference C for the same informal problem N (plan 2.1):

Source Defective original D Corrected reference C Informal input N
miniF2F the v1 Lean 4 statement (DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5's minif2f.jsonl) the miniF2F-v2s statement of the same name the v1 informal statement
ProofNet the original Lean 4 port (DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5's proofnet.jsonl) the ProofNet# statement the original informal statement

An item is a candidate if D and C differ after normalisation: 228 of 488 miniF2F items and 147 of the 369 matched ProofNet items (D1 item 5).

Lean filters (plan 2.2; Lean 4.9 with DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5's Mathlib, autoImplicit off). Both statements must elaborate, and they must not be certified equivalent in both directions by the tactic portfolio (20 s per direction). Every pair that survived elaborated in Lean 4.9, the first environment, so the Lean 4.15 fallback was never used.

Blind defect tagging (plan 2.3). Two raters tagged all 289 kept pairs: a Claude Opus 5.5 subagent (rater A) and a Claude Fable 5.1 subagent (rater B). Each saw only the informal statement and the two formal statements, labelled A and B in a seeded random order, with the theorem name replaced by X. Neither knew which statement was the original.

  • Agreement on whether D is faithful: Cohen's κ = 0.957.
  • Included (both raters: D unfaithful and C faithful): 129 items, 46 miniF2F and 83 ProofNet.
  • One-rater set (exactly one rater says so): 6 items.
  • The raters gave the same defect category to 120 of the 129 included items (93%).

Twins and paraphrases

Number-perturbed twins (plan 3.1). A builder subagent (Claude Opus 5.5) was given N, D, C and the raters' defect reasons. It changed the problem's numbers consistently in all three, recomputed the answer with a script, and carried the defect over to the twin's defective statement D′. For example:

N: What is the smallest integer greater than 10 that is both a perfect square and a perfect cube? Show that it is 64.

D: (n : ℕ) (h₀ : 10 ≤ n) (h₁ : ∃ x, x ^ 2 = n) (h₂ : ∃ t, t ^ 3 = n) : 64 ≤ n. This is only a lower bound: it does not say that 64 qualifies.

N′: What is the smallest integer greater than 100 that is both a perfect square and a perfect cube? Show that it is 729.

D′: (n : ℕ) (h₀ : 100 ≤ n) (h₁ : ∃ x, x ^ 2 = n) (h₂ : ∃ t, t ^ 3 = n) : 729 ≤ n.

A twin was kept only if it passed five scripted checks (plan 3.1):

  1. D′ and the corrected twin C′ elaborate.
  2. D′ and C′ are not certified equivalent, so the defect survives.
  3. The three diffs (N→N′, D→D′, C→C′) change only numerals, apart from a declared answer span.
  4. The answer script reruns to the stated answer.
  5. The portfolio cannot refute C′.

Twin attrition.

  • Builders built 63 twins and declined 72 items, almost all because the problem has no numerical parameter to change. That is true of most ProofNet items, which are abstract statements about groups, rings and spaces: 64 of the 83 included ProofNet items have no twin.
  • All 63 built twins passed all five checks.
  • The primary set is the 59 included items with a valid twin: 40 miniF2F and 19 ProofNet. It is above the plan's floor of 40 items, below which the power calculation would not hold.
  • Four more twins belong to the one-rater set (sensitivity analysis).

Meaning-preserving paraphrases (plan 3.2; secondary). Another subagent reworded every one of the 135 informal statements, keeping every number, object and claim. A blind Fable 5.1 judge, shown the two texts in random order, judged all 135 to state the same problem.

Negative controls. These are the eight miniF2F items whose v2 edit introduced a defect (minif2f-v2-audit, v2_miscorrection). Here D is the defective v2s statement and C the faithful v1 statement. No model in the study can have seen v2, so outputting those defects estimates the natural rate of those particular mistakes.

The catalogue was committed and frozen (commit d0f4aea) before any model was sampled.

Classifying outputs

Each output's statement was extracted (the last Lean code block, after any thinking) and checked in Lean against the condition's references (D and C for originals and paraphrases; D′ and C′ for twins):

  • defect copy: certified equivalent to D (or D′) in both directions;
  • correct: certified equivalent to C (or C′);
  • other: elaborates, but is equivalent to neither;
  • does not elaborate: includes outputs with no statement.

Identical statements within an item and condition were checked once (19,807 unique statements).

The test

The test follows plan section 6. It is computed over the three primary models' temperature samples on the 59 primary items:

  • x_O is the number of defect copies on originals;
  • x_T is the number of analogous defects on twins;
  • each is out of n = 59 × 3 × 16 = 2,832.

RR = ((x_O + 0.5) / n) / ((x_T + 0.5) / n), with a 95% item-cluster bootstrap interval (2,000 draws).

  • Supported: RR ≥ 3 and a lower bound above 1.5.
  • Refuted: an upper bound below 3.
  • Inconclusive: otherwise, or if x_O + x_T < 20.

Weakened conclusions dominate. Half of the primary items, and just over half of all defect outputs (225 of 429), are "weakened conclusion" defects. Typically a "find the least (or greatest) value" problem is stated as a one-sided bound. That is the single most common pattern in miniF2F v1's defects, and the models write it just as readily for the twin.

The "wrong constant" row is one item. Its interval is degenerate (1.00-1.00) because that one item has identical counts in the two conditions, so every bootstrap draw does. A ratio of 1 does not mean "no copying" here: 8.3.6a is the clearest case of a memorized statement in the study (next section).

ProofNet. Its lower bound of 1.01 is barely above 1 and rests on 19 items whose counts are nearly identical in the two conditions. Its ratio, 1.07, is far from the hypothesised 3. The 19 items come from eight sources (seven textbooks and the Putnam), and eight of them come from abstract algebra (Dummit-Foote, Artin, Herstein).

Kimina reproduces the ProofNet port's templates (exploratory; found by the review, D8)

Why the twins cannot see it. The twin design assumes that a memorized statement is recalled together with its numbers. Suppose instead a model has memorized a statement as a template and fills in whatever numbers it is given. It then produces the analogous defect on the twin just as often, so RR ≈ 1 by construction. The independent review found that Kimina does this. Three observations show it.

1. The port's header lines. The ProofNet Lean 4 port starts each textbook's statements with fixed open lines: for example open Fintype Subgroup Set Polynomial Ideal for Dummit-Foote, and open Topology Filter Real Complex TopologicalSpace Finset for Rudin. Nothing in an informal statement asks for them. The table gives the share of extracted temperature samples whose open lines include every line of the item's port header:

Model ProofNet items without a twin (64): O P Items with a twin (19): O T P
Kimina-Autoformalizer-7B 54% (547 of 1,022) 51% 40% (121 of 304) 37% 38%
Goedel, Herald, DeepSeek-Prover-V2, Qwen 0% 0% 0% 0% 0%
  • Rate by split. On originals the rate is about the same for ProofNet's test items (48%) as for its validation items (53%).
  • Which header. Kimina usually writes the header of the item's own textbook:
  • Rudin items: 167 of 224 outputs;
  • Munkres items: 113 of 144;
  • Shakarchi items: 32 of 32.

For algebra items it sometimes writes another algebra textbook's header instead. On Dummit-Foote items it wrote the Dummit-Foote header 175 times and the Herstein header 87 times (header_by_textbook.csv). - The same with new numbers. The rate is about the same on originals, twins and paraphrases of the same items.

2. A constant only the port contains. Dummit-Foote 8.3.6a reads "Prove that the quotient ring Z[i]/(1+i) is a field of order 2".

  • The port's error. The port writes the ideal as span ({⟨0, 1⟩} : Set GaussianInt), the ideal generated by i. Since i is a unit, the quotient is the zero ring and the statement is false.
  • On the original. Kimina writes exactly this, with the port's header, in 11 of 16 samples.
  • On the twin, "Z[i]/(2+i) is a field of order 5", it writes ⟨0, 1⟩ again in 11 of 16 samples and changes only the order, to 5. Nothing in the twin's text yields ⟨0, 1⟩. The reviewer confirmed in Lean that this output is equivalent to D′, and that an output with the right generator ⟨2, 1⟩ is equivalent to C′.
  • On the paraphrase it writes ⟨0, 1⟩ in 5 of 16 samples.

3. The port's statement bodies. Dummit-Foote 4.5.13, 4.5.14, 4.5.15 and 4.5.17 ask for "a normal Sylow p-subgroup for some prime p dividing its order". For these, Kimina writes the port's body ∃ (p : ℕ) (P : Sylow p G), P.Normal, which drops "p prime" and "p divides |G|". It does so 42 times on originals and 39 times on twins.

Where the copies sit. Kimina's copies are almost all on ProofNet items: 80 of its 86 on originals and 74 of its 81 on twins. Most of Herald's (93 of 111) are on miniF2F items, where the defects are the one-sided bounds and dropped conditions shown above. There is no comparable fingerprint for miniF2F: its header, open BigOperators Real Nat Topology Rat, is shared by many training sets.

What this shows and what it does not. Kimina's training data evidently included the ProofNet port's statements, or data copied from them. The header depends only on the textbook, so it cannot show which items were seen. The 8.3.6a constant is item-specific, but that item is in ProofNet's validation split, which a model may legitimately train on.

Where the port entered training data. The study does not know Kimina's training data item by item. One documented route by which the port entered the open training lineage is the Lean Workbook pipeline. Its first translation model was trained on all miniF2F and ProofNet statement pairs, both splits, the ProofNet pairs taken from this same port (arXiv 2406.03847, section 4.1).

What drives it, item by item (results/tables/review/per_item_OTP.csv):

  • Kimina's Sylow items. On Dummit-Foote 4.5.14 and 4.5.15, its copies fall from 15 and 12 on originals (15 and 9 on twins) to 1 and 1 on paraphrases. The paraphrases keep the same content: 4.5.14's reads "possesses, for at least one prime p dividing |G|, a Sylow p-subgroup that is normal". So clearer content does not explain the drop. With the original wording, numbers changed or not, Kimina writes the port's statement body; with new wording, it mostly writes a correct one. Together with the port fingerprint, which is about as common on paraphrases as on originals, this favours wording-keyed recall of the port's statements for Kimina's ProofNet items.
  • Herald. Part of its drop is an artefact of what elaborates. On amc12a_2021_p7, "the least possible value of (xy−1)² + (x+y)²", it writes (xy - 1) in 10 of 16 paraphrase samples, against 1 of 16 on the original. Lean reads xy as an unknown identifier, so those outputs do not elaborate.
  • Not uniform. On some items paraphrases draw more copies than originals, for example mathd_algebra_410 (5 on the original, 12 on the paraphrase).
  • The paraphrase check is weak. The paraphrase judge accepted all 135 paraphrases. A judge that accepts everything gives no evidence that it can tell a changed problem from an unchanged one.

Accuracy against the defective reference

On the 59 primary items, the three primary models' outputs are certified correct (≡ C) in 13.5% of samples on originals and 13.4% on twins. They are equivalent to the defective reference (≡ D) in 7.2% and 7.9%. So scoring against the v1 or original-port statement would credit about 7% of samples for reproducing its defect. That credit is as large on twins as on originals, so it does not depend on recall tied to the numbers.

Negative control: defects no model can have seen

The eight control items carry a defect that miniF2F-v2's edit introduced; their faithful statement is the v1 one.

  • Herald reproduced the v2 defect in 11 of its 128 samples on these items. Eight of those are on mathd_algebra_393, "if f(x) = 4x³ + 1, find f⁻¹(33)", whose v2s statement writes f⁻¹ 33. In Lean that is the pointwise reciprocal 1/f(33), not the inverse function.
  • DeepSeek-Prover-V2 reproduced a v2 defect once.
  • Kimina, Goedel and Qwen never did.
  • Outputs equivalent to the faithful v1 statement were rare on these eight items: 1 of 640 samples. Several of these v1 statements use idiosyncratic forms (σ : Equiv ℝ ℝ), and elaboration on these items is low (Herald 85 of 128, Qwen 75 of 128).

So a natural pitfall can reproduce even a defect that no model can have memorized, at least for Herald.

What this means

The fingerprint does not fire, in the form tested. The hypothesis treated reproduced benchmark defects as a fingerprint of memorized benchmark statements. Open autoformalizers do reproduce those defects, in 7-8% of samples, and in nearly every sample on some items. But they reproduce them just as often when the problem's numbers are changed. So the defects are not recalled together with the problem's numbers.

Two different things give RR ≈ 1, and the design cannot tell them apart.

  • Shared formalization habits. These are one-sided bounds for "least" or "greatest" problems, and dropped positivity conditions. Most of Herald's copies are of this kind, on miniF2F. The negative control shows that such a pitfall reproduces even a defect no model can have seen.
  • Template recall. Here a memorized statement has its numbers filled in afresh. Kimina does this on ProofNet: the port's headers, its Sylow statement bodies, and its wrong constant on 8.3.6a, on twins as on originals.

The header fingerprint and the 8.3.6a constant separate the two for Kimina. Nothing in this study separates them for Herald's miniF2F copies.

The miniF2F-v2 paper (arXiv 2511.03108) says the decline it reports "suggests that current autoformalizers may suffer from data contamination". Its human-judged formalization accuracy, from v1 to v2s:

Model Test Validation
Kimina 88.1% → 79.5% 82.0% → 78.7%
Herald 55.3% → 51.6% 59.4% → 48.0%
o4-mini 46.3% → 51.6% (rises) 47.1% → 52.5% (rises)

This study bears on that in two ways:

  • On miniF2F, where most of Herald's copies are, the defects are copied as often on twins, so there they look like shared habits. A v1 reference credits those habits whether or not the model has seen the benchmark.
  • On ProofNet, Kimina's reproduction of the port is the kind of contamination the paper suspects.

The study has no miniF2F counterpart of the header fingerprint, so it cannot say whether Kimina's miniF2F accuracy is affected the same way.

What a better test would need. The twins keep the original wording, so they detect only recall tied to the numbers. The paraphrase comparison points to wording-keyed recall for Kimina, but paraphrases could also change difficulty, and the judge did not check that. A sharper design would use:

  • format fingerprints such as the port's headers;
  • twins that also change the wording;
  • paraphrases matched for explicitness.

Best of all would be the models' training-data provenance.

Limitations

  • Twins exist only for problems with numbers, and this biases the primary set towards RR ≈ 1. 59 of the 129 included items have a valid twin, but only 19 of the 83 included ProofNet items do.
  • What was excluded. The 64 ProofNet items without a twin are abstract statements, where Kimina's port fingerprint is strongest: 54% of its outputs on those items, against 40% on the items with a twin.
  • What was kept. The primary set keeps miniF2F's "find the value" problems, whose defect, a one-sided bound, is the most natural pitfall of all.
  • The result. The primary set leans towards items where RR ≈ 1 is expected under both readings. The result speaks mostly to problems with numerical parameters.
  • Few items carry the signal. Defect outputs come from 27 items. The item-cluster bootstrap accounts for this, but per-model and per-category intervals rest on a handful of items. Several intervals have a degenerate bound (1.00) because every defect output in that subset sits on one or two items with equal counts in both conditions.
  • Machine raters, builders and judges. The defect tags come from two LLM raters (κ 0.957 on D), not from the benchmark authors.
  • Some "defects" are faithfulness gaps that leave the statement true. Putnam 2010 A4 without n > 0 is still true at n = 0, for example. Those are among the defects most naturally reproduced.
  • Twins were built by an LLM and checked by script, not by hand.
  • The paraphrase judge accepted every paraphrase.
  • Twin texts keep the source's formats. On the AIME twin of aime_1988_p3, Herald read the three-digit answer "016" as 0.16 in 8 of 16 samples. That item's counts (7 on the original, 4 on the twin) partly reflect this.
  • Equivalence is certified by a tactic portfolio with a 20 s limit. Equivalent outputs the portfolio cannot connect are counted as "other", in either condition. A normalising re-check of near-identical outputs changes little (D8), but misses beyond its reach remain possible.
  • One environment. Lean 4.9 with DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5's Mathlib. Models trained on newer Mathlib produce some statements that do not elaborate there, in every condition alike.
  • Five open 7-8B models. Closed or larger autoformalizers were not tested.

Independent review and what changed

An independent Claude Fable 5.1 reviewer (review/review.md) recommended fix first, with 17 issues.

What it confirmed.

  • It recomputed every number from the classification files and found them right.
  • It confirmed that each stage's code and the frozen catalogue were committed before the outcomes they could affect.
  • It read about 40 "copy" and 40 "other" outputs by hand and found no wrong "copy".

What it found. The draft overstated what the result means. Its headline said the reproduced defects were "habits, not recall of the benchmark's statement". But the study's own data show Kimina reproducing the ProofNet port as a template, which the twin design classifies as a habit.

What changed.

  • Scope. The verdict, the plain-terms summary and "What this means" now limit the result to recall tied to the numbers.
  • New results.
  • a section on Kimina's template recall, with the header fingerprint and 8.3.6a;
  • the per-item O/T/P comparison, and Kimina's O vs P ratio on the 59 items (1.99);
  • a corrected reading of the paraphrase result: the 4.5.14 example had been cited for the wrong reading;
  • the certifier's missed equivalences, with a normalising re-check;
  • the 97 outputs equivalent to both references;
  • D6 applied to the O vs P set;
  • the D6 timeline, and the post-hoc status of the flagged-twin sensitivity;
  • the selection bias's direction;
  • the twin answer-format quirk.
  • Narrower claims. The negative-control claim is narrowed to the eight items.

Two review points were checked against the sources and corrected.

  • The size of the miniF2F-v2 drop. The reviewer called it small (Herald −0.8 pp). That figure comes from the paper's other table, which scores Herald's automated judge. The paper's sentence about contamination refers to the formalization-accuracy table quoted above, where the drops are 3-11 points.
  • The Lean Workbook premise. The reviewer said plan section 1's premise was inaccurate. The Lean Workbook paper does train its first translator on all miniF2F and ProofNet pairs, so the premise holds. Its wording is made precise in D8.

The revised report went back to the same reviewer for a confirmation pass (review/confirmation.md).
