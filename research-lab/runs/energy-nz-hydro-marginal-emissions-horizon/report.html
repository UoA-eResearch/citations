How much carbon does an extra megawatt-hour cost New Zealand, and when?

Horizon-resolved marginal emissions for a hydro-dominated grid. The data are ten years of half-hourly metered generation, an emissions ledger calibrated to the Ministry for the Environment's grid factors, and cold-weather demand as the instrument. The question is whether carbon-aware load shifting cuts emissions.

Verdict: Inconclusive. All three preregistered hypotheses are Inconclusive.

  • H1: the weather instrument has no strength at the 30-day horizon it needed.
  • H2:
    • the daily scheduler puts its load where the instrument is weak;
    • the time-of-day construct the plan chose cannot answer the question even where the instrument is strong.
  • H3: storage state makes no detectable difference.

What holds in every specification we tried (17 control sets, instruments and subsamples):

  • The carbon cost of extra demand grows with the time over which it is counted.
    • MEF(7 d) exceeds MEF(1 h) in every specification, by 0.12 to 0.64 t CO2/MWh.
    • MEF(1 d) exceeds MEF(1 h) by 0.04 to 0.24.
    • Under the preregistered specification, the factor is 0.15 t/MWh over an hour (95% CI 0.13–0.17), 0.31 over a day (0.27–0.37) and 0.69 over a week (0.54–0.94).
    • The levels are not robust. Across specifications, the one-hour factor ranges from −0.16 to 0.23 and the one-week factor from 0.13 to 0.79. The levels depend chiefly on whether earlier demand and emissions are controlled.
  • The average emissions factor that carbon-aware tools read hardly moves through the day. It is 0.095–0.100 t/MWh in every 6-hour block, against a period mean of 0.098.
    • Moving a 20 MW, 4-hour daily load to the day's lowest-AEF hours claims only 0.014 t CO2 per MWh actually moved, by the AEF's own accounting.
    • So whichever marginal factor is right, AEF-guided intraday shifting has very little to claim.

What we cannot say:

  • Why the factor rises. Two explanations fit the data equally:
    • water used now is made up with fossil generation later;
    • cold weeks are also dry weeks, so operators conserve water. The instrument predicts lower inflow over the window, and controlling for it shrinks the one-week factor to 0.55.
  • Which fuel responds. The coal/gas split changes sign between reasonable control sets.
  • Whether the AEF scheduler captures less than half of its claim (H2). No scoring gives a positive, well-identified capture. One alternative instrument even gives a negative realised saving.

In plain terms

Some software, data centres and smart appliances now try to run "when the grid is clean". They read a signal of the grid's average carbon intensity and move work to the hours when it is lowest.

New Zealand's grid is mostly hydro, with some coal, gas and geothermal. We wanted to know whether moving electricity use around in time actually reduces emissions there.

We built a half-hourly record of the carbon each power station emitted from 2015 to 2024. We then used cold snaps, which push demand up for reasons nobody chose, to see what happens to emissions when demand rises.

Two things came out clearly.

First, the extra emissions build up over days. The carbon cost of extra demand is smaller counted over the next hour than over the next day, and smaller over a day than over a week. That held in every version of the analysis we tried. But the size of the effect depends a lot on modelling choices: counted over a week, anywhere from about 1.3 to 8 times the grid's average intensity. We also cannot tell whether the lakes are being drawn down and refilled with fossil power later, or whether cold weather simply tends to come with dry weather.

Second, the "clean hours" signal barely changes through the day. So shifting work from one hour to another has almost nothing to gain by its own measure.

We could not test precisely how much of that small claimed saving is real.

What was asked

Carbon-aware schedulers usually consume an average emissions factor (AEF). In a hydro-dominated grid, the lakes balance most short-run changes in demand. So an hour-to-hour shift may simply move water around, and any emissions it causes arrive when thermal plant later fills the gap.

The study estimates the marginal emissions factor (MEF) as a function of the horizon over which emissions are counted. It then asks three preregistered questions (plan.md):

  • H1: Is MEF(30 d) larger than MEF(1 h)?
  • H2: Does an AEF-guided daily scheduler capture less than half of its claimed saving?
  • H3: Is the 1-day MEF higher when national hydro storage is low?

What was already known

The novelty search (2026-10-18) found no published horizon-resolved MEF for New Zealand.

  • Electricity Authority. It reports which fuel is marginal (hydro or thermal about 95% of the time) and publishes an allocation factor that is not a marginal one.
  • Merit-order price effects. A 2026 Energies paper studies state-dependent price effects in this market, not emissions.
  • Reservoir hydro elsewhere. WattTime explains why reservoir hydro moves emissions in time. Hydrothermal marginal CO2 modelling exists for Chile (Applied Energy, 2017).

How it was done

Data, all open, 2015-01-01 to 2024-12-31 (local); 175,344 half-hours, of which 173,569 have full lags and 30 days of future data: - Generation. EMI Generation_MD, every metered unit by trading period. - Smelter and HVDC. The Tiwai smelter offtake and HVDC transfers. - Hydrology. The Electricity Authority's Hydrological Modelling Dataset: - Storage. Daily active storage for ten lakes, in GWh via the dataset's specific energy. - This is not the Authority's "controlled storage" series. For four southern lakes the HMD range is about twice the scheme's active capacity. - Each offset is constant, so the day-of-year anomaly and the standardised control are unaffected. - Inflows. Eight natural inflow series plus Wānaka's natural outflow, energy-weighted. - Temperature. Open-Meteo (ERA5) hourly temperature for five cities, population-weighted.

The emissions ledger (code/series.py): - Each thermal unit's metered output is multiplied by a stated heat rate and the MfE fuel factor. Geothermal output is taken at 0.10 t CO2/MWh, which is 15–24% of ledger CO2 each year. - Each year, fossil emissions are scaled to MfE's annual grid-average CO2 factor (the "Electricity Used" rows of the MfE emission-factor file). - This is deviation D1. MBIE's quarterly tables, named in the plan, block scripted download. - The scale factors are 0.87–1.10. - Against MfE's quarterly factors for 2023–2024, the within-year shape matches (RMSE 0.006 t/MWh, against a quarterly spread of 0.03). The annual calibration forces the yearly mean, so this tests the shape only. - The demand measure is total metered generation. It tracks 2024 grid offtake almost perfectly (r = 0.997; it is 9% larger because it includes embedded generation and losses).

The estimator (code/mef.py): - Regression. For each horizon h (30 minutes to 30 days), emissions summed over the next h are regressed on demand summed over the same window, by 2SLS. - Instrument. The population-weighted heating-degree anomaly in the starting half-hour. - Controls (as preregistered): - the previous day's anomalies; - the previous half-hour's and previous day's demand and emissions; - storage, and the week's inflow; - the previous day's HVDC and Tiwai; - wind over the same window; - fixed effects for half-hour by day type, month and year. - Inference. Intervals come from a calendar-month block bootstrap: 999 resamples of 120 months. - Rule. A horizon whose first-stage F is below 10 is weakly identified, and a hypothesis resting on it is Inconclusive.

After the independent review (deviation D2), code/sensitivity.py re-estimates the main quantities under 17 alternative specifications. These are exploratory and not preregistered.

Does AEF-guided shifting capture its claimed saving? (H2)

The scheduler. - A 20 MW load runs 10:00–14:00 by default. - Each calendar day, the scheduler moves it to the 8 consecutive half-hours of lowest AEF. - Because the "24-hour window" is the calendar day, 15% of chosen windows start at midnight. - 29.5% of days overlap the default window, so 250,640 of the 292,240 MWh of load is actually moved.

The claimed saving. 3,618 t over ten years: 0.014 t per MWh moved (0.012 per MWh of load).

The plan's realised saving. It uses "time-of-day MEFs at 1 day": the 1-day MEF of windows starting in each 6-hour block, estimated separately per block. - 06:00–12:00: 0.22 (0.15–0.28). - 12:00–18:00: 0.27 (0.22–0.32). - 18:00–24:00: 0.40 (0.29–0.58). - 00:00–06:00: F = 4.2, point estimate −0.37, interval reaching −3.8.

41% of the moved load lands in 00:00–06:00.

H2: Inconclusive. Four things must be read together:

  1. The construct cannot answer H2. A block "MEF" is a property of how a 1-day window is aligned. It is not the marginal factor of load placed in that block. Applying the four values as if they were block-specific factors is what the plan specified, but the resulting "realised saving" does not measure the question even with a strong instrument.
  2. The weak-block rule was set after the night block's F was known. The plan's weak-instrument rule is stated for horizons. We extended it to blocks when coding the simulation, after mef.json showed the night block's F = 4.2 (deviation D2).
    • Under the plan's literal rule, the capture ratio of 18 (0.83–127) has a lower bound above 0.5, and H2 would read Refuted.
    • That ratio is meaningless: it is driven by the weak block's negative point estimate.
  3. With a stronger instrument the answer flips sign. The night block is weak because of the half-hour instrument. With the calendar-day mean anomaly as instrument, all four blocks are strongly identified: 0.30, 0.23, 0.28 and 0.33 (F 83–387).
    • The preregistered scoring then gives a negative realised saving: −8,853 t, a capture of −2.45 (−4.05 to −0.99). The scheduler moves load into the night, whose window MEF is higher than midday's.
    • Without the lagged-outcome controls, the same rescoring is uninformative.
  4. No scoring shows a positive, well-identified capture.

The plan's other outputs: - The share of days on which the realised saving has the opposite sign from the claimed one is 0.36. This uses the weak night block. - The ±1-hour variant moves only 65,220 MWh, 22% of the load: the scheduled window overlaps the default by 6 of 8 half-hours on average. Its realised saving is simply the 06–12 versus 12–18 difference applied to load crossing noon. The capture of 0.34 (−0.08 to 0.75) is uninformative.

Storage state (H3)

H3: Inconclusive. The 1-day MEF is 0.30 (0.21–0.40) in the lowest storage tercile and 0.32 (0.25–0.39) in the highest. The low-minus-high difference is −0.02 (−0.14 to 0.11). All terciles are well identified (F 26–40).

Other checks

  • Geothermal placebo. Geothermal should not respond.
    • It responds by 0.04–0.06 MWh/MWh from 12 hours to 2 days. Intervals are near zero at their lower ends; at 2 days it is 0.004–0.12.
    • The plan's flag ("interval excludes ±0.05") is ambiguous. Read as "excludes zero", it fires at 2 days.
    • A direct supply channel exists. Air-cooled binary geothermal plants produce more in cold air, so the instrument moves some supply directly. Gas turbines behave the same way.
    • The emissions consequence is about 0.006 t/MWh.
  • The wind control is material: without it, MEF(1 d) is 0.40 rather than 0.31. Wind curtailment, rare before 2023, does not drive the result: 2020–2024 gives 0.39.
  • Tiwai (descriptive only). One smelter demand-response episode, 9–19 August 2024, meets the rule: daily offtake at least 100 MW below its trailing 30-day median for at least 7 days. The plan said "drops of at least 100 MW sustained for at least 7 days".
    • During it, thermal output was 29.9 GWh a day, against 31.5 in the month before.
    • These episodes are called in tight, dry periods by design, so they cannot be read causally.

What this does and does not show

  • It shows two things.
    • In this grid, the emissions consequence of extra weather-driven demand is larger counted over days than over hours, under every specification tried.
    • The average emissions factor varies by less than ±3% through the day, so it gives an intraday scheduler almost nothing to act on.
  • It does not establish the size of the marginal factor. The one-week value lies anywhere from 0.13 to 0.79 t/MWh depending on defensible control choices. "Several times the average" holds only for some specifications.
  • It does not establish the mechanism. Water replacement and cold-dry co-occurrence cannot be separated with this instrument, and the fuel composition is not stable.
  • It does not answer H2. The preregistered construct measures window alignment rather than time-of-day marginal emissions. The instrument is weak at night. With a stronger instrument the scored saving is negative, and that depends on the control set.
  • The ledger. Heat rates are stated assumptions, and calibration is annual. A uniform scale error cancels in the gradient's sign.
  • Scope. National totals, 2015–2024.

Process and deviations

  • Preregistration. The plan was committed before any series was downloaded (df14d01).
  • D1 was recorded before any emissions series was computed:
    • annual MfE calibration instead of MBIE quarterly;
    • stated ledger assumptions;
    • energy-weighted inflows;
    • approximate population weights.
  • The independent review (review/review.md) recommended "fix first". It reproduced every number from raw data with independent code. It showed that the levels, mechanism and composition in the pre-review draft were not robust, and that H2 needed fuller disclosure.
  • D2 records the post-review work:
    • the 17-specification sensitivity analysis;
    • the post hoc block-level weak-instrument rule;
    • the H2 construct limitation;
    • the H1 instrument mismatch;
    • the storage-level caveat.

The preregistered pipeline and its numbers are unchanged. - Implementation notes: - The time-of-day MEFs are four separate regressions with all controls re-partialled within each block. This is equivalent to the plan's fully interacted design. - The descoped NEM placebo was replaced by the geothermal placebo.
