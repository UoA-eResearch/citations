Does co-folding affinity know its target?

A kinase-panel selectivity test of Boltz-2: hold each ligand fixed, vary the protein, and compare with measured binding constants.

Report · 2026-10-13 · pre-review draft Preregistration Deviations log Code All research leads

Verdict: Refuted. The preregistered hypothesis — that Boltz-2's affinity head cannot rank proteins for a fixed ligand — does not hold. It ranks them with a median correlation of 0.44, puts a true top-3 kinase first for 56 % of compounds, and never falls below zero on any of them. It also does not demonstrably beat a baseline that ignores the compound entirely on the primary statistic, captures barely a third of the compound-by-kinase interaction that selectivity consists of, and reports differences between kinases at about half their true size.

In plain terms

Drug companies need to know not just whether a compound hits its target, but what else it hits. A drug that blocks the intended enzyme and thirty others causes side effects. So the practical question for any new affinity predictor is: given a compound, can it tell you which proteins that compound will bind?

Boltz-2 is an open model that predicts binding affinity roughly a thousand times faster than the physics calculations it aims to replace. We tested it the way a drug project would: take 32 known kinase inhibitors, ask the model to rank 32 human kinases for each one, and compare with laboratory measurements of all 1,024 combinations.

We expected it to fail, and preregistered that prediction. It didn't. The model's rankings correlate about 0.44 with the measurements, well above the threshold we had set for failure.

But a correlation on its own means little without something to compare it against, so we also wrote down, before running the model, what some deliberately stupid methods achieve. One of them ignores the compound entirely and simply ranks kinases by how many drugs bind them in general. That scores 0.39 — statistically indistinguishable from the model.

The resolution is that the two methods fail differently. The stupid baseline is excellent for promiscuous compounds and actively wrong for selective ones, dropping as low as −0.42. The model is never actively wrong on any of the 32 compounds, and when asked to name a compound's single best target it gets one right 56 % of the time, against 19 % for the baseline.

Where it falls short is the thing selectivity actually consists of. Most of what varies in real measurements is the specific pairing of compound and kinase; the model captures only about a third of that, leaning instead on how potent a compound is overall. It also flattens the kinome: the difference it reports between any two kinases is about half the real one, whether they are near-twins or unrelated proteins. So it will point you at roughly the right targets while understating how much they differ.

Useful for triage, then, and better than nothing by a clear margin at the top of the list. Not yet a substitute for the experiment when two close relatives have to be told apart.

What was asked

Boltz-2 predicts a binding affinity for a protein–ligand pair at a thousandth of the cost of a free-energy calculation, and its affinity head is the headline open claim of 2025–2026. People are beginning to use it for selectivity: given a compound, which of many proteins will it hit? That use needs the model to respond to the protein, not only to the chemistry of the ligand.

The published audits test the axis the authors emphasised — rank a series of ligands against one target — and agreement with physics-based free energies on a handful of systems. This study tests the other axis. On a complete kinase profiling panel (Davis et al. 2011, as packaged by DeepDTA: 68 inhibitors against 442 kinase assays, every cell measured), it holds each ligand fixed and asks the model to rank kinases, then compares that ranking with the measured dissociation constants.

The preregistered hypothesis, in plan.md, was that the model fails this test: median within-ligand across-kinase Spearman ≤ 0.25, while still clearing 0.4 on the conventional axis.

How it was done

The panel. A 32 × 32 rectangle of the Davis panel, every cell predicted. Kinases had to be wild-type, non-phosphorylated Davis entries whose sequence matches a reviewed human UniProt entry exactly, with exactly one annotated protein-kinase domain of 220–350 residues; 303 of 442 qualified, and 32 were taken by largest-remainder allocation across the eight Manning groups, choosing inside a group by the number of uncensored Davis cells. The model input is that kinase domain plus a 20-residue flank — full-length multi-domain kinases are neither affordable at panel scale nor what the competition binding assay reports. Ligands had to be RDKit-parseable, single-fragment, MW ≤ 700 and have at least 8 uncensored cells among the chosen kinases; the 32 taken are recognisable kinase drugs (pazopanib, crizotinib, gefitinib, erlotinib, sorafenib, sunitinib, bosutinib, axitinib, neratinib, vandetanib, fedratinib, staurosporine and the rest).

Selecting on uncensored counts matters: 69.6 % of the whole Davis matrix sits at the 10 µM assay ceiling, but only 27.1 % of this rectangle does, and the median ligand has 22 uncensored cells out of 32. The selectivity question is therefore asked where there is a real dynamic range to rank. The cost is a sample of relatively promiscuous kinases and ligands, which a separate arm probes.

The model. Boltz-2 2.2.1 with its released weights, in affinity mode, one protein chain plus one ligand chain, one MSA per protein from the public ColabFold server, reused across every complex that protein appears in. Settings are the defaults, passed explicitly: 3 recycling steps, 200 sampling steps, 1 diffusion sample, 5 affinity diffusion samples, 200 affinity sampling steps, no molecular-weight correction, seed 20261013 plus the shard index. The read-out is affinity_pred_value, which is log10(IC50 in µM), converted to a pIC50-like scale (6 − value) so that higher means stronger, matching pKd; affinity_probability_binary is reported alongside. Note that the molecular-weight correction cannot change any within-ligand statistic, because for a fixed ligand it is a constant.

Two consequences for reading the hypothesis. The threshold it set on the selectivity axis, 0.25, is below what a baseline with no chemistry in it achieves (0.386): knowing only which kinases bind most compounds gets you a correlation of 0.39 with a given compound's real selectivity profile. And the floor it set on the conventional axis, 0.4, is below what a baseline with no protein in it achieves (0.489): ranking ligands within a target is mostly ranking ligands by overall potency. The verdict below follows the preregistered rules, but the baselines are the more informative comparison, which is why they were preregistered too.

The measured panel's variance splits 25.5 % to the ligand, 14.6 % to the kinase and 59.9 % to the ligand-by-kinase interaction — so most of what a selectivity predictor has to get right is the interaction, not either main effect.

Results

All 1,024 panel complexes predicted, no failures, no missing cells. The secondary arms add 573 more, for 1,597 in total — the preregistered budget exactly.

The preregistered verdict: Refuted

The hypothesis was that the model fails this test, with a median within-ligand Spearman of 0.25 or below. It does not fail it.

The lower end of every interval sits above 0.25, so by the rule in plan.md §6 the hypothesis is refuted: the affinity head is not protein-blind, and its rankings are not noise. Censoring is not doing the work — dropping the ceiling cells moves P1 from 0.444 to 0.464 — and neither is structural quality, since the high-confidence half scores slightly higher than the whole.

The two statistics disagree, and the disagreement is the result. On the preregistered primary statistic — the median rank correlation over the whole 32-kinase profile — Boltz-2 is within noise of a baseline that never looks at the compound. On finding the right targets it is not: it puts a genuine top-3 kinase first for 56 % of ligands, against 19 % for the promiscuity baseline and 9 % for chance. The hit-rate comparison was not preregistered with an interval; that was added on seeing the gap, and is reported as the addition it is (D8).

Two things explain the gap. The profile contains a 27 % block of cells tied at the assay ceiling plus a long tail of weak binders, where ordering carries little information and the model adds little; the median Spearman weights all of that equally with the top. And the per-ligand floor differs sharply, which is visible in F1 and is an exploratory observation (D9):

worst ligand ligands below 0 ligands below 0.25
Boltz-2 +0.029 0 of 32 3 of 32
B1 promiscuity −0.387 2 of 32 7 of 32
B2 neighbour −0.419 3 of 32 11 of 32

The baselines reach their median by being excellent for promiscuous compounds and actively misleading for selective ones. Boltz-2 is never actively misleading on this panel. For a tool meant to nominate a handful of targets, that floor matters more than the median.

What the model gets wrong: it hears the ligand, and it flattens the kinome

The two-way decomposition says where the error lives (F3). In the measurements, 60 % of the variance is the ligand-by-kinase interaction — which is exactly what selectivity is. In the predictions it is 35 %, while the ligand main effect swells from 25 % to 53 %.

share of variance measured predicted
ligand (which compound is potent in general) 25.5 % 53.4 %
kinase (which kinase binds most things) 14.6 % 11.4 %
ligand × kinase interaction (selectivity itself) 59.9 % 35.2 %

The predictions are also compressed: the spread of a ligand's profile is half the measured spread (SD 0.52 against 1.00), and across the whole panel 0.76 against 1.19. So differences are in the right direction more often than not, but systematically too small.

The geometry arm (exploratory, D3) says what the model is listening to. Taking each kinase's 32-ligand prediction vector, removing ligand means and comparing the resulting kinase-by-kinase distance matrices:

predicted distances vs kinase-domain sequence distance 0.338 0.0003

The model has a real picture of which kinases resemble each other, and it is not a sequence lookup: it agrees with the measured geometry (0.434) more than with sequence distance (0.338), on a panel whose median pairwise crop identity is only 23 %.

An earlier draft of this report claimed the model tracks sequence more tightly than the measurements themselves do (0.338 against 0.248). A kinase bootstrap of that difference does not support it: +0.081 (−0.099, +0.284), positive in 80 % of draws. The claim has been withdrawn.

What the geometry does show is flattening. Across all 496 kinase pairs the median measured difference is 1.12 log units and the median predicted difference 0.56 — a ratio of 0.50. The natural suspicion is that close paralogues are flattened worst, since that is the case drug projects care about, but the panel's five most similar pairs give ratios of 0.33 to 0.79, median 0.57, which is no worse than the panel at large:

So the compression is uniform rather than paralogue-specific: the model halves the distance between any two kinases, whether they are near-twins or unrelated. That is still the thing to know before using it to separate two close relatives — the differences it reports will be about half their true size — but it is a calibration failure across the kinome, not a specific blindness to paralogues.

The secondary arms

Decoys (128 complexes). For every one of the four non-kinase proteins, the median prediction sits 1.1 to 1.3 log units below the same ligand's mean across the 32 kinases — 2.0 to 2.4 within-kinome standard deviations. No decoy ever outscored a ligand's best kinase (0 of 128), and only 2 of 128 outscored a ligand's kinase mean. The binary probability separates them further still (median 0.07 – 0.29 against 0.44 for kinases). Kinase inhibitors are recognised as kinase binders; this is the easy discrimination, and the model passes it cleanly.

Promiscuity probe (256 complexes). Adding the eight least-promiscuous eligible kinases to the panel leaves the result where it was: P1 = 0.475 (0.359 – 0.573) over the 40-kinase union. The eight on their own give 0.284 (0.192 – 0.577), but with an average of 1.4 uncensored cells per ligand that sub-rectangle is too censored to carry any weight, and it is reported rather than relied on.

Affinity sampling (96 complexes re-predicted). Doubling the affinity sampling steps and running nine diffusion samples instead of five changes nothing: predictions correlate 0.979 between settings, move 0.13 log units on average, and P1 shifts by −0.029 (−0.206, +0.091). The result is not a sampling artefact.

Post-cutoff compounds (93 complexes). The eleven compounds whose earliest ChEMBL document is 2025 or later, profiled against 6 to 12 kinases each (61 kinases in all, 3 % of records censored), give P1 = 0.543 (0.371 – 0.657) — if anything above the Davis panel's 0.444, with intervals that overlap heavily. Whatever the model is doing, it is not reciting Davis.

Three caveats keep this a check rather than a replication. The measurements are ChEMBL pActivity pooled over mixed assay types rather than one laboratory's Kd, so some of the within-compound variation is between-assay noise. Six to twelve kinases make a per-compound correlation noisy. And this arm admits no baseline: the promiscuity analogue needs kinases measured across several compounds, but only 8 of the 61 appear in three or more, leaving the baseline defined for 4 compounds over 3 to 6 kinases each — two of which share all six, so their "mean over other compounds" is essentially each other and both score exactly 1.0. That is an artefact of the overlap, and it is reported as not computable rather than as a comparison. Ninety-three pairs is the ceiling of what public post-cutoff kinase profiling currently offers.

Training support (no GPU). Across the panel's kinases, pre-2024 ChEMBL compound counts span 91 to 5,205, and accuracy barely tracks them: Spearman 0.147 against the within-kinase correlation, 0.251 against pairwise concordance, −0.041 against absolute error. On this panel the model is not simply reciting the best-measured targets.

The other head is better

One practical finding worth stating separately. Boltz-2 emits a binary binder probability alongside the affinity value, and on this axis the probability is consistently the better ranker: P1 = 0.596 against 0.444, within-kinase 0.614 against 0.453, and a Mantel correlation of 0.520 against 0.434. Anyone using Boltz-2 for selectivity triage should be ranking by affinity_probability_binary, not by affinity_pred_value. That comparison was preregistered as a secondary read-out, so it is not a fishing expedition, but the size of the gap was not anticipated.

What this does and does not show

The headline is easy to misread in either direction, so stated plainly: on this panel Boltz-2's affinity head carries real protein-side signal — it is not ranking kinases at random, and it clears the preregistered threshold comfortably — but on the preregistered primary statistic it does not demonstrably beat a baseline that never looks at the compound, and its advantage is concentrated at the top of the ranking and in its floor rather than spread across the profile. The hypothesis this study set out to test was refuted, and the threshold it set was, as the preregistration itself anticipated, too low to be the interesting comparison. The interesting comparison is the baseline table, and it was fixed before any prediction was made.

A reader should not take "Refuted" to mean the model is good at selectivity. It means the specific claim that it is useless at selectivity is wrong. The limitations below were written from the design, before the predictions were analysed.

It tests one axis, not the model. The question is whether a fixed ligand's predicted ranking of proteins tracks the measured one. That is the axis selectivity work needs, and it is not the axis Boltz-2 was tuned or benchmarked on. A model can be genuinely useful for ranking a congeneric series against one target — the published audits say it is — and carry no usable protein-side signal. Nothing here speaks to pose accuracy, to the structure module, or to the conventional axis beyond the one within-kinase number reported for scale.

One model version, one read-out. Boltz-2 2.2.1 released weights, the affinity_pred_value head, the default settings passed explicitly. The binary binder probability is reported alongside because it is a differently-trained head on the same features; the sampling arm varies the diffusion budget. A later release could behave differently, which is why the decoy protocol and panel definition are released with the report rather than only the numbers.

The crop is a real assumption. The model sees the annotated kinase domain plus 20 residues, not the full-length protein. That matches what the competition binding assay reports and makes a panel affordable, but accessory domains, linkers and partners that shape selectivity in the cell are absent. If removing them is what removes the signal, that is a finding about the input the model can be given at scale, not about the kinome. Crop length and Boltz's own confidence scores are reported per complex, and the primary statistic is recomputed on the high-confidence half, so a reader can check whether the failures are the poorly-folded inputs.

Training exposure cuts one way. Davis is from 2011 and is almost certainly inside the training distribution. That makes a null result harder to explain away — the model is not being asked to extrapolate — and it makes a positive result uninterpretable as generalisation. The post-cutoff arm (11 compounds first published in 2025, 93 pairs) and the training-support gradient bound this, but 93 pairs is the ceiling of what public post-cutoff profiling offers, so that arm is a check, not a replication.

Censoring is handled two ways and both are reported. 27.1 % of the rectangle sits at the 10 µM assay ceiling. P1 keeps those cells as ties at pKd 5, because telling binders from non-binders is part of selectivity; P2 drops them and asks only about ordering among real numbers. The verdict follows P1, as preregistered.

The panel is deliberately the easy case. Kinases and ligands were chosen for having many uncensored measurements, which buys dynamic range to rank but samples the more promiscuous end of both. The promiscuity probe adds the eight least-promiscuous eligible kinases to test whether the picture changes where binding is rare. A panel chosen for difficulty would be a harder test, not an easier one.

Rank, not calibration. Davis reports Kd from a competition binding assay; the model's head mixes Kd/Ki/IC50. Every primary statistic is a rank correlation within a fixed ligand, which is invariant to any monotone recalibration — including the molecular-weight correction, which for a fixed ligand is a constant and cannot change a within-ligand statistic at all. Absolute agreement in log units is reported descriptively and should not be read as a calibration claim.

No docking comparison. A Vina or Glide baseline would answer "is this better than the cheap physical alternative?", which is a different and larger study; it was declared out of scope in the plan (§5.8) rather than dropped quietly. The baselines here are deliberately trivial — target promiscuity, nearest chemical neighbour, ligand-only, molecular weight — because the point of the comparison is what a model must beat to be worth running at all.

Reproducing it

Everything needed is in the run directory, and nothing in it requires a credential: the Davis panel comes from the DeepDTA release, the kinase sequences from UniProt, the alignments from the public ColabFold server, and the model weights from the Boltz release.

code/panel.py                  select the 32 x 32 rectangle   -> data/panel.json
code/validate_data.py          check the matrix against the paper and PubChem
code/build_inputs.py <arm>     write one YAML per complex     -> data/inputs_<arm>/
code/boltzrun.py msa <seqs>    fetch one MSA per protein      -> data/msa/
code/boltzrun.py run <in> <out>  boltz predict, resumable
code/boltzrun.py collect <out> <arm> results/predictions.jsonl
code/assemble.py tidy          per-pair tables                -> results/tables/pairs_<arm>.csv
code/assemble.py primary       P1-P5, baselines, verdict      -> results/tables/primary.json
code/secondary.py              decoy, promiscuity, sampling, support
code/geometry.py               sequence-vs-prediction geometry
code/figures.py                F1-F5
code/test_analysis.py          17 checks on the estimators

The estimator checks are worth running first: they assert that a perfect predictor scores 1, that a ligand-only predictor scores exactly 0 on the selectivity axis, that the sign convention is right, that a pure-noise predictor's interval covers 0, and that each of the four verdict branches fires on data built to trigger it. They take a second and they caught two real bugs in this analysis.

Seeds and settings are fixed in code/boltzrun.py (SEED = 20261013, the explicit flag dictionary) and code/analysis.py (SEED, NBOOT = 2000). results/tables/env.json carries the package versions and the sha256 of both released checkpoints, so a rerun can confirm it used the same weights. The per-complex read-outs, with Boltz's own confidence scores, are released as results/predictions.jsonl; the alignments themselves are not redistributed, only the manifest of which sequence was aligned.

One operational note for anyone repeating this at panel scale. A thousand complexes is about fifteen GPU-hours, which is long enough that the machine will probably interrupt you: this run was stopped twice by host crashes. Boltz writes each complex's outputs as it finishes and skips on restart any complex that already has them, so both interruptions cost one complex each. Fetch the alignments once per protein and cache them, rather than letting the predictor fetch per complex — the same 32 proteins appear in every column of the rectangle, and the public server is the slowest step in the whole pipeline by an order of magnitude.
